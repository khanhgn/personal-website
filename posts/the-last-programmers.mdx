## I. The Complaint

<p className="lede">In the <em>Phaedrus</em>, Socrates tells a story about the invention of writing. A god offers it to the king of Egypt as a gift for memory, and the king is unimpressed. People will stop remembering, he says. They will seem to know many things while knowing almost nothing.</p>

Every generation makes some version of this complaint about the next. Usually the complaint loses. Writing gave us law, science and this essay. But the king wasn't entirely wrong either. We did lose a kind of memory. We just decided the trade was worth it, and then forgot there had been a trade.

So the question is never whether something gets lost. Something always does. The question is whether it was load-bearing.

I started university in 2023 and had about a year of learning to program the old way before I started leaning on ChatGPT. These days I don't write code by hand anymore. That puts me on the seam between two ways of learning it, close enough to feel what changed, which is why I want to ask the king's question about programming.

## II. Cost and Friction

The first programmers learned on machines that charged them for everything. Memory, cycles, compile time. You thought before you typed, because a careless loop could cost you an hour. Their taste came from cost, the way you learn the price of things when you don't have much money.

The next generation had garbage collectors, frameworks and Stack Overflow. The first generation said they had it easy, and in one sense they did. But they paid somewhere else. They wrote enormous amounts of bad code and debugged it themselves, and that was the education. The machine stopped charging for resources and started charging for mistakes.

That is where a senior engineer's instinct comes from. They look at a diff and know it's wrong before they can say why, because they wrote that exact wrong thing years ago and lost an afternoon to it. You can't write that down. It's scar tissue.

## III. The Seam

I got about a year of scar tissue. I resisted ChatGPT for a while, the way you resist anything that feels like cheating. Then I used it, because everyone did and it worked.

Now I describe problems to agents and judge what comes back, and I feel pretty guilty about it. When the agent writes something I wouldn't have, I sometimes go back and read it until I could have written it myself. That habit is what I got from that year, and it's what I'm most afraid of losing.

The problem is that the line has blurred. I used to know which things I understood and which I was taking on trust. Now there's a big middle: code I've read, nodded at and shipped. Some of it I understand. Some of it I've just seen so many times that it feels like understanding. From the inside I can't always tell which is which.

It's worse with tests. More and more, I let the agent write the tests too, and then I let it tell me they pass. The thing checking the work is the thing that did the work. I know that's backwards, and I do it anyway, because it's usually fine.

## IV. Plausible

The next generation will barely write code by hand. They'll learn by prompting, and prompting mostly works. That's the problem.

When the first attempt mostly works, there's no error to read and no afternoon lost. You can get very far without ever building a model of how the thing works. They'll be productive from their first week. And many of them won't know why their code is right, because they never had to find out why it was wrong.

The usual answer is that this has happened before. Almost nobody reads assembly anymore, and compilers didn't make us worse engineers. We moved up a level. Maybe this is the same.

I don't think it is, yet. A compiler is deterministic and almost always correct, so you could stop reading its output. An agent's output is plausible, which is a much weaker property. It's what correct code tends to look like.

<Plausible />

Sometimes plausible code is wrong, like version a. The stranger case is when it's right. Philosophers call it a Gettier case when you're right by luck: you believe something true, for a reason that isn't what makes it true. A program that works for reasons nobody understands is like that. Call it <Term id="gettier-code">Gettier code</Term>. You can't see it, because nothing is wrong. Not until it breaks and nobody can fix it.

Pilots know a version of this. Autopilot made flying much safer. But when it gives up, it gives up at the worst possible moment, and hands the plane to pilots who rarely fly that part by hand.

<Autopilot />

Someone still has to read the code. If almost everyone can write it and almost no one can read it, the few who can read end up vouching for everyone else.

## V. Taste

Most of my good instincts are mistakes I made often enough to stop making.

If the agent makes the mistakes and fixes them, I don't compress anything. The model already did, in training, on other people's mistakes. It has the taste. I get the output.

Maybe that's fine, and the next generation's taste will just be about different things: what to build, what to refuse, how to say exactly what you mean. But look at what failure costs at each layer. A wrong loop costs an afternoon. A wrong product costs a year. You get thousands of the first kind in a career and maybe a dozen of the second.

<Samples />

Taste needs a lot of samples. I don't think a dozen is enough.

## VI. What Was in the Work

There's a second loss, and it isn't about competence.

We assumed the tedious part of programming stood between us and the meaningful part. The agents took the tedious part, and it turns out a lot of the meaning was in it. An afternoon spent on a bug was one of the few times you met something completely indifferent to what you wanted. The machine didn't care what you meant. It did exactly what you said, and you had to change until what you said was right.

<Indifferent />

The agents are the opposite. They try to give you what you meant. That's what makes them useful, and it might be what makes them bad teachers.

When a skill stops being necessary, it doesn't disappear. It becomes something you do on purpose. Nobody needed a gym when work was physical. Maybe programming gets its gyms too: an afternoon with the agent switched off, being wrong on purpose.

The title is too strong. There will be programmers after us. But "the last" can mean the last people to learn it a particular way: slowly, by being wrong, alone with a machine that didn't care. I got a year of that. The generation after me will do what I do, earlier and faster, and won't know there was anything to skip.

<Debt />

I keep thinking about all the Gettier code we're about to ship. It will run, it will be fine, and someone will depend on it. Whether what we lost was load-bearing, we'll find out the way people usually find out about load-bearing walls.
